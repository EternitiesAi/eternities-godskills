# Research proposal development

This method helps a research team turn a specific funding opportunity into a coherent local proposal draft. It does not certify eligibility, sponsor compliance, budget allowability, scientific merit, award likelihood, or institutional approval. A human with the appropriate authority owns all commitments and submission actions.

## Use and boundaries

Use when a named research-funding opportunity needs a fit assessment, proposal outline, narrative, workplan, budget assumptions, or internal review. Start only from information the user supplied or explicitly authorized you to access. If the controlling opportunity document or institutional requirements are unavailable, mark affected requirements `unknown` and request the smallest missing source; do not fill the gap from remembered agency rules.

Do not invent results, citations, investigator roles, partner commitments, approvals, facility access, preliminary evidence, eligibility, cost rates, deadlines, page limits, or formatting rules. Distinguish completed work from proposed work. A draft is not a submission, and a polished narrative is not evidence that the plan is feasible.

## 1. Bind the opportunity

Create a short source ledger for the exact opportunity and applicant. For each governing document, record its identity, issuing authority, version or date, access date, section or page locator, and the proposal requirement it supports. Prefer the controlling solicitation and application guide over summaries. Record institutional policy or grants-office instructions separately; when requirements conflict, preserve both and ask the responsible office rather than choosing silently.

Build a finite requirement table with one row per independently checkable obligation:

| Field | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Requirement | Eligibility, narrative element, attachment, budget rule, review criterion, or submission step |
| Authority and locator | Exact document identity, revision, section/page, and access date |
| Owner | Person or office responsible for the answer or artifact |
| Evidence or artifact | Source data, draft section, form, approval, or commitment that satisfies it |
| State | `met`, `missing`, `conflicted`, `unknown`, or `not applicable` with the reason |

Do not convert a suggestion into a mandatory rule or treat a missing instruction as permission. Keep a visible list of hard fit blockers, internal deadlines, unanswered policy questions, and decisions that belong to the principal investigator or institution.

## 2. Decide whether to pursue the opportunity

Summarize the applicant, research question, intended beneficiaries, opportunity purpose, and required contribution. Compare the proposed work with the opportunity’s stated scope and eligibility using cited evidence. Identify the strongest reason to proceed and the strongest reason not to proceed. If eligibility, timing, institutional participation, or a required capability is unresolved, state the hold and its decision owner; do not label the opportunity a fit by default.

## 3. Construct the research argument

Write a compact logic chain before expanding prose:

1. What important question or barrier is in scope, and for whom?
2. What is known, what remains uncertain, and what supplied evidence supports that distinction?
3. What objective or testable question would the project address?
4. Which aims or work packages answer it, and how will each be assessed?
5. What outcomes would support, weaken, or redirect the proposed explanation?
6. What change in knowledge, capability, practice, education, or public benefit is plausible if the work succeeds—and what is outside the claim?

For every material claim, label its basis as established evidence, preliminary observation, inference, hypothesis, proposed activity, or intended future benefit. Attach source locators to factual claims. Keep uncertainty, contradictory findings, and negative results visible. Do not describe a goal as an expected result unless the evidence supports that confidence.

## 4. Make the plan feasible and assessable

For each aim, connect the rationale to the approach, inputs, comparison or control, outcome measure, analysis, dependencies, deliverable, and a decision point. Explain how the design can distinguish the outcomes that matter. State sample or data needs, quality controls, analytic assumptions, and limits only where the supplied methods support them. Route specialized statistical, clinical, regulatory, animal, human-subject, security, or engineering questions to their proper owner; this method does not certify them.

Build a workplan that shows sequence, owners, dependencies, milestones, evidence of completion, and contingency paths. Tie each claimed capability to a named person, resource, or documented arrangement. Separate confirmed collaboration from a request for future collaboration. A letter not yet obtained is not a commitment.

## 5. Align resources and budget assumptions

Map each requested resource to a task, quantity or effort basis, period, and source. Use current institutional rates and the opportunity’s current cost rules only when those sources are available and authorized. Show assumptions and unresolved amounts rather than hiding them in totals. Check that staffing, equipment, data access, travel, subawards, indirect costs, and milestones tell the same story. This is not financial approval or permission to commit funds.

## 6. Draft to the actual review criteria

Use the opportunity’s current required structure; do not import a generic agency template or remembered page/format rules. Map each review criterion to the evidence and proposal section that addresses it. Draft the core scientific argument first, then the supporting sections and summary. Keep terminology, aims, timeline, budget, team roles, and promised outputs consistent across every attachment.

Use only supplied or authorized material. Identify missing references, data, permissions, approvals, and accessibility needs. Figures are optional aids unless the controlling opportunity explicitly requires them; use only authorized assets and do not fabricate data or imply that a generated illustration is measured evidence.

## 7. Challenge the draft before handoff

Run separate, bounded reviews:

- **Scientific logic:** Can a skeptical reader trace the question to the aims, design, measures, and interpretable outcomes?
- **Feasibility:** Are workload, dependencies, staff, time, and resources credible? Are the likely failure modes and alternatives explicit?
- **Evidence and claims:** Is each factual or preliminary claim supported by a traceable source? Are proposed outcomes clearly distinguished from observed results?
- **Opportunity fit:** Does each current review criterion map to a substantive response rather than a keyword or generic promise?
- **Cross-document consistency:** Do names, dates, effort, amounts, aims, milestones, and commitments agree across the narrative, forms, and budget?
- **Compliance readiness:** Does each requirement have an authoritative source, locator, owner, artifact, and state? Leave unresolved items open.

Preserve reviewer disagreements and proposed edits separately. A self-check is not independent scientific, institutional, legal, or sponsor review.

## Return and stop

Return the opportunity/source ledger, fit decision and blockers, requirement crosswalk, argument outline or draft, workplan, budget assumptions, review findings, unresolved decisions, and the human owner for every commitment or submission. Stop at a reviewable local artifact unless the user separately authorizes further access or an external action. Before an authorized submission, the responsible institution and applicant must confirm the final governing instructions and approve the package.
