# Product discovery and prioritization

Use when choosing which product problems to investigate, which opportunities to
pursue, or how to revise a backlog or roadmap under constraints. The outcome is
a defensible choice among feasible alternatives, including learning first or
keeping the present plan. A routine status update, already-decided feature
specification, isolated customer reply, or delivery-process redesign needs a
different method.

Scale the work to the decision. For two inexpensive reversible options, a short
comparison and one next action may suffice. Use a portfolio table or dependency
map when shared prerequisites, scarce resources, commitments, or material
uncertainty can change the choice. No scoring framework, meeting format, or
estimation unit is required.

## Method

1. **Frame the choice.** Identify the people and outcome affected, decision
   horizon, constraints, existing commitments, and who can decide. Separate a
   hard deadline from a desired date. Preserve the current plan as an alternative;
   specify what new evidence or constraint would justify changing it.
2. **Separate the reasoning.** An observed problem is a difficulty supported by
   evidence. An opportunity is a hypothesis about a worthwhile improvement for
   a defined group. A solution is one possible intervention. An experiment is
   an action intended to reduce a particular uncertainty. Link these explicitly:
   a requested feature is evidence of a request, not proof of the problem's
   prevalence, the solution's effectiveness, or a commercial outcome. Consider
   alternative solutions and an option that requires no new feature.
3. **Judge evidence for this horizon.** Attach relevant source, observation time,
   segment, conditions, denominator when known, and contradictions to consequential
   claims. Distinguish observations, estimates, and assumptions. Ask whether the
   evidence transfers to the intended users, current product, and decision period.
   Recent feedback can be narrow; older evidence can remain relevant when its
   conditions persist. Missing evidence remains unknown. See
   [methods.md](references/methods.md) for evidence and experiment mechanics.
4. **Establish feasibility before ordering.** Expand each option to the work and
   conditions required to deliver its intended outcome: unresolved research,
   approvals, enabling work, external inputs, release and support effort. Mark
   each prerequisite satisfied, incomplete, or unknown, with its owner and closure
   evidence. Estimate remaining work by skill, resource, and time window; include
   waiting and sequencing separately from active effort. Past spending is not
   remaining effort. Reconcile effective capacity after existing commitments and
   known obligations without counting the same deduction twice. A skill shortage
   or missing approval cannot be overcome by spare aggregate hours.
5. **Compare feasible portfolios.** For combinations of options, count shared
   prerequisites once and check that each resource and dependency fits the horizon.
   Compare outcomes, uncertainty, risk, delay, and reversibility using compatible
   units; keep incomparable benefits separate. Make trade-offs explicit instead
   of hiding them in a weighted score. A score may summarize a stated judgment,
   but cannot establish feasibility or invent missing quantities. Show what
   plausible changes in estimates would reverse the choice.
6. **Choose learning when it can change the decision.** Select a bounded, cheap,
   reversible test of the uncertainty that matters most to the feasible options.
   State the claim, observation, success and failure signals, inconclusive outcome,
   time and resource budget, stop rule, and decision that follows. Count experiment
   effort against the same capacity. Do not extend a weak test indefinitely or
   interpret preference feedback as causal evidence of retention or revenue.
7. **Revise commitments honestly.** When new work arrives, compare the old and
   proposed portfolios using remaining effort, interruption cost, and downstream
   effects. Identify what is displaced, narrowed, delayed, or explicitly funded;
   an addition may use actual slack without displacing anything. Distinguish
   recommendation from authorized commitment. In a Now/Next/Later view, Now holds
   accepted active commitments, Next holds conditional candidates with entry
   conditions, and Later holds directional opportunities with revisit triggers.
   None implies an unsupported delivery date. A blocked commitment stays visible.

## Deliverable and finish

Return the selected option or portfolio, its evidence and feasibility rationale,
the main sensitivity, affected commitments, and the next owner or discriminating
test. A concise decision note is enough when it contains what the decision needs.
For a roadmap revision, show the material change and conditions for Next to enter
Now. Distinguish planned tests from performed tests and expected outcomes from
observed outcomes.

Finish when the choice can be acted on within its authority and resource limits,
or when a specific unresolved prerequisite explains why commitment must wait.
This method does not itself authorize customer contact, private-data acquisition,
procurement, implementation, pricing changes, publication, or external promises.
