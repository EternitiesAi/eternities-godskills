# Evidence, feasible portfolios, and changes

## Keep the problem distinct from the proposed answer

A report that people cannot finish a task can support a problem statement when
its source and conditions are known. A proposal to add an assistant is a solution
candidate. The opportunity might instead be reducing the work needed to finish
that task. Testing clearer instructions, changing a prerequisite, or doing nothing
can be legitimate alternatives. Preserve the link between evidence and the claim
it actually supports; several requests from one account are not several independent
accounts, and enthusiastic interviewees are not an observed conversion rate.

Use the smallest useful evidence comparison. For a consequential choice, consider
coverage of the affected group, relevance to the decision period, selection bias,
measurement conditions, contradictory observations, and what remains unobserved.
Name the strongest unsupported link between the problem, opportunity, solution,
and expected outcome. This is often more useful than assigning a confidence
percentage with no calibration.

An old observation becomes weaker when the product, population, workflow, or
external conditions change. A new observation does not automatically override a
broader, well-supported pattern. If a past result came from another segment,
state the transfer assumption and test it when it can affect the choice. An
urgent deadline changes the cost of waiting; it does not increase evidence quality.

## Close prerequisites and price remaining work

For each candidate, follow prerequisites until reaching something already
available or an unresolved condition that blocks the outcome. Include knowledge,
authorization, technical, operational, and external dependencies as relevant.
Record whether the condition is actually satisfied and how that was established.
A named owner or promised delivery is not closure evidence. A cycle requires a
different sequence, a smaller independently useful slice, or a blocked disposition.

Define the outcome boundary before estimating. A prototype may answer a research
question while leaving release, training, support, migration, or recovery work
unfinished. Count that work only for the outcome being compared, but do not label
a prototype estimate as the cost of delivering the production outcome. A partial
solution is an alternative only if its limited outcome has value and its limits
are visible.

Estimate remaining effort in units the team can support. Separate effort from
elapsed waiting time, unavailable resources, and uncertain approvals. Historical
effort is relevant to learning and interruption cost, not a reason to continue
an unattractive option. A partly completed item may be cheap to finish, expensive
to interrupt, or obsolete; inspect its current state rather than favoring or
discarding it merely because work has started.

Model effective capacity by relevant skill or resource and time window. Reconcile
staffed availability with absences, existing commitments, recurring duties, and
evidenced interruption needs. Avoid a generic productivity factor that subtracts
the same obligations again. Historical throughput can inform estimates when work
mix and staffing are comparable; velocity or story points are not universal effort
units. Shared reviewers, testing environments, equipment, budget, or specialist
skills may constrain a portfolio independently of total available labor.

## Compare combinations, not only individual scores

For a candidate set S, let K(S) be S together with all incomplete prerequisites
needed for its selected outcomes. In each skill/resource window, add the remaining
demand from K(S), counting genuinely shared work once. Compare this with effective
capacity. Also check dependency order, waiting time, exclusive alternatives,
deadlines, and authority. Passing an aggregate sum is necessary only where that
sum models the constraint; it does not prove that a schedule exists.

For a small decision, compare a few materially distinct combinations: current
commitments, a narrow useful outcome, a shared enabler plus dependent work, and
learning before commitment. Avoid enumerating a huge backlog merely for completeness.
When choices interact, say how: shared cost, mutually exclusive solution, overlapping
benefit, or work that unlocks later options. Do not sum benefits measured in
incompatible units or count the same affected group twice.

Illustrative example: two workflow improvements each need four specialist-days,
and both need the same eight-day preparation. Together they need sixteen days,
not twenty-four. The preparation must still finish early enough for both improvements.
If only twelve days remain, the pair cannot be promised; preparation plus one
improvement, a narrower outcome, or deferral may fit. These invented quantities
explain the calculation, not a capacity recommendation.

A second constraint may bind: five days of analysis for one option and three for
another exceed six available analyst-days, even with abundant engineering time.
Replacing the analyst with an unqualified person is a new feasibility assumption,
not free capacity. Shared preparation may unlock several options, but its value
depends on whether those options remain useful and feasible within the horizon.

Compare options that satisfy the hard constraints on the outcomes the decision
owner values. An option is dominated only when another is at least as good on
every relevant dimension and better on one, under the same assumptions. Overlapping
ranges or incomparable outcomes call for a trade-off or a test, not fabricated
precision. If weights are supplied, show their meaning and sensitivity; changing
a weight is a value judgment, not new product evidence.

## Use uncertainty to choose a test

Vary the assumptions that could realistically change the choice: remaining
effort, closure date of an external dependency, eligible population, benefit
range, resource availability, and cost of delay. Keep linked assumptions coherent;
do not combine incompatible best cases. Report whether the choice is robust,
conditional on a named threshold, or unresolved. Do not invent probability
distributions merely to produce an expected-value figure.

Choose a test only when its possible results would change an action. Define what
would count against the favored solution as carefully as what would support it.
Bound exposure, time, resources, reversibility, and the observation window. A
prototype walkthrough can reveal whether a workflow is understandable; it cannot
by itself establish adoption, sustained use, or incremental revenue. A small
sample with no detected failure is not proof that failure is absent.

If every possible result leaves the same choice, the test may be unnecessary or
aimed at the wrong uncertainty. If evidence will arrive after the decision must
be made, choose a reversible action or explicitly accept the unresolved risk.
Use specialist research or statistical methods when causal outcomes or consequential
inference require them; this method organizes the product choice.

## Displacement and honest horizons

For incoming work, ask what changed and which claim or constraint it affects.
Compare a baseline with the proposed revision, including lost work, switching,
restart, and affected dependencies. Preserve obligations until the responsible
owner changes them. If actual slack absorbs the addition, show why it fits;
otherwise name the displaced outcome and the cost of that change. Do not keep
both promises against the same constrained capacity.

Now contains accepted active work, including explicitly bounded discovery work.
Keep a blocked Now item visible until its owner revises the commitment. Next
requires entry conditions such as a test result, prerequisite closure, released
capacity, or an approval. Later states a possible direction and what would make
it worth revisiting; it need not imply eventual delivery. Dates, owners, and scope
should be as precise as the evidence and authority support. External communication
or tracker mutation requires the task's actual authorization.

Before finishing a consequential recommendation, inspect whether unsupported
feedback became a prevalence or revenue claim, a shared prerequisite was counted
twice, a scarce skill was hidden in aggregate hours, an unknown dependency became
a promised date, a sensitivity changes the choice, or an addition silently keeps
the displaced commitment. For a simple reversible choice, check only the failure
modes that could matter.
