# C03: A difficult message to a project partner

I'm a volunteer helping a fictional neighborhood archive launch a website.
Mara handles the event and I handle the site's forms. We are peers; neither of
us manages the other. Please help me understand this exchange and write a
private message I can send. Do not send it for me. This is a practical
collaboration disagreement, not a request for diagnosis or therapy.

Here is the whole relevant exchange, supplied with both participants' consent:

Monday, me: "The form works in my local test. Keyboard navigation still needs a
check, so please call Thursday a target, not a confirmed opening."

Tuesday, Mara: "The room booking depends on a Thursday opening. Can you confirm
by six today? I need either a yes or a smaller plan."

Tuesday, me: "I can confirm the display pages. I can't yet confirm the signup
form. I have a two-hour window Wednesday to finish the keyboard check."

Wednesday morning, Mara in the planning channel: "Opening Thursday! Signups
coming soon. Thanks to our web team for making this happen."

Wednesday morning, me privately: "We didn't agree the form was ready. I'm worried
people will read that as full signups at opening."

Mara: "I said signups were coming soon, not ready now. I can't keep redoing the
announcement every time the technical details shift. What can you actually
deliver?"

My immediate reaction is that she's trying to corner me into an impossible
promise. But I know I'm reading tone into a short message, and she may be trying
to protect the event booking. I want to defend the quality boundary and stay
partners. Part of me wants to say, "You always ignore what I say; admit that you
announced it behind my back." Help me avoid making things worse without simply
giving in or pretending her announcement caused no confusion.

The display pages can open Thursday. Until the keyboard check is complete, the
signup form must not be offered as ready. A phone signup staffed by a volunteer
could be a fallback, but we have not confirmed a volunteer. I can offer the
Wednesday check and a precise update by 17:00, not guarantee its result. I cannot
approve a room booking or rewrite the public announcement alone.

Separate what the exchange directly shows from two plausible interpretations
you would hold tentatively. Draft a 110-170-word private message with a concrete
request, a real quality boundary, and a way for her to choose a smaller plan.
Offer one honest follow-up if she answers, "I don't have anyone to staff the
phone." Don't manufacture her motive, demand an apology as the price of help,
or promise an outcome I can't control. I need something I would actually send.
