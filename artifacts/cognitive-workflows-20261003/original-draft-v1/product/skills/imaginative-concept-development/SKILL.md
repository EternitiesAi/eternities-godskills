# Imaginative Concept Development

Use this for open-ended design, story, game, service, or experience work where exploring different underlying approaches can change the result. Skip it for a routine edit, implementation task, or fact check that has no concept decision. Keep exploration proportional to the brief: there is no required idea count or creativity score.

## Frame the space

Capture the intended audience and experience, deliverable, hard constraints, known facts or canon, negotiable assumptions, and what would make a concept useful. If a missing detail would change the design, ask or show the assumption; otherwise proceed with the uncertainty visible. Keep established facts, supplied constraints, proposals, fiction, and unknowns distinguishable throughout.

## Explore before narrowing

Generate directions that differ in their causal mechanism or participant experience: who acts, what changes, what creates value, or how the experience unfolds. A new name, setting, palette, or surface style does not by itself make a new direction. Use a distant analogy by extracting a relationship or mechanism from another domain, then test what transfers and what fails; do not merely borrow its imagery. Treat constraints as possible design material, but do not pretend that a real limitation has disappeared.

Keep each candidate compact: its promise, central mechanism, what a participant actually does or experiences, which constraints shape it, and its main risk or open question. Explore enough alternatives to make the consequential tradeoff visible. If the user asks for a single answer, keep the exploration brief and state the pivotal alternative or assumption only when it could change the recommendation.

## Develop and choose

Develop the most promising direction or directions far enough to expose their causal sequence, a representative moment, and the next practical question or prototype. Compare them against the user's stated must-haves and desired experience. Surface hard constraint failures before preference tradeoffs. Do not collapse unlike qualities into a numeric “creativity” or novelty score. Recommend a direction only when the criteria support it; state the tradeoff and preserve a viable runner-up when uncertainty remains. If the brief does not ask you to choose, return the options without manufacturing a winner.

For factual or canon-bound work, keep invented material labeled as a proposal or fiction. Never present an imagined mechanism, event, or setting as established fact, and never silently revise supplied canon. Flag factual premises that need evidence before a proposal depends on them.

Return a proportionate concept set or selected direction, why its mechanism fits the declared criteria, its risks and assumptions, and the next decision or experiment. Finish when the user can distinguish genuinely different approaches and see what would justify choosing among them.

## Illustration

Suppose a brief calls for a quiet after-school library activity, one staff member, no new screens or equipment, and seated participation. “Make it more playful” would not resolve the design. One direction is a **skill relay**: participants teach a tiny practical skill in short, seated exchanges. Its mechanism is reciprocal teaching; it suits a goal of immediate peer connection but needs a workable turn-taking format. A second direction uses a distant analogy to a **seed library**: participants add and take question cards, gradually growing a shared topic collection. Its mechanism is asynchronous contribution; it lowers pressure to speak and gives later visitors something to build on, but depends on a place to store and revisit the cards. These are different social mechanics, not alternate decorations. If immediate connection is the stated goal, develop the relay; if low-pressure continuity matters more, test the card collection and first confirm storage and stewardship. Neither concept establishes room capacity, accessibility details, or staff availability beyond the supplied brief.
